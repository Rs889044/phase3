\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the final evaluation of the proposed framework. All numbers
are produced by the reproducible pipeline (data preparation, feature extraction,
training, evaluation, deployment profiling) on the cleaned datasets. Where a
measured outcome differs from the interim report, the difference is stated and
explained.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}

All accuracy results use the protocol defined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation} (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_strategy}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NASA leave-one-cell-out (LOCO):} all four folds. In each fold
    one cell is held out for \emph{test} and a second cell is held out for
    \emph{validation} (early stopping and model selection); the model is trained
    on the remaining two cells. Each fold is repeated over \textbf{3 random
    seeds}. Scalers and physics priors are fit on the \emph{training cells only}
    to prevent leakage.
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset generalization:} train on the full NASA set,
    evaluate zero-shot on CALCE CS2\_35. As established in
    Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}, both datasets are LiCoO$_2$
    (LCO); this is therefore a \emph{cross-dataset / cross-load /
    cross-form-factor} test, not a cross-chemistry one.
    \item \textbf{Reporting:} SOH RMSE is reported as the mean over folds$\times$seeds
    with a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval (1000 resamples); model comparisons
    are tested with the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.
\end{itemize}

Seven learned architectures are compared: \textbf{two Transformers} --- a
\emph{light}, edge-sized model (17k parameters, hyperparameter-tuned like every
other baseline) and a \emph{heavy}, SOTA-scale model (225k parameters, the
state-of-the-art accuracy reference representing the attention models reported in
the literature) --- an LSTM, a vanilla GRU, a continuous-time PIML baseline (Neural-ODE),
and the proposed hybrid in its two physics variants: \textbf{route~A} (fixed
closed-form Verhulst/double-exponential prior used as a soft penalty) and
\textbf{route~B} (a \emph{learnable} ODE-residual whose parameters are trained
jointly with the network). They are measured against the two non-learned
reference floors of Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}: the empirical
double-exponential curve fit and a non-recurrent SVR.

\label{sec:physics_interpretability}

trajectories (training cells only). Table~\ref{tab:prior_fits} reports the

\textbf{(1) The two-timescale prior is justified.} The double-exponential model
achieves a lower AIC than the logistic on \emph{every} cell ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$
from $-86$ to $-347$, always favouring double-exp), with a higher $R^2$ and a
smaller residual spread on each cell (Figure~\ref{fig:redundancy}). This answers
the redundancy question between the two priors: the two-timescale decay (fast SEI
stabilisation plus slow active-material loss) describes the data better than the
single logistic S-curve, so the double-exponential is retained as the primary
closed-form prior and the logistic as an alternative configuration.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{2-mainmatter/chapters/redundancy.png}
    \caption{Closed-form prior comparison per cell. (Left) the double-exponential
    attains a higher fit quality ($R^2$) than the logistic on every cell; (Right)
    it also produces a smaller residual spread. This justifies retaining the
    two-timescale prior alongside the logistic.}
    \label{fig:redundancy}
\end{figure}

\textbf{(2) A fixed prior cannot describe atypical cells.} Cell B0006 begins
\emph{above} rated capacity (initial SOH $\approx101.8\%$) and exhibits the
heaviest capacity regeneration. Figure~\ref{fig:fit_b0006} shows that even the
flexible double-exponential, when fit to B0006, struggles with its early
super-rated bulge --- and a prior fit on the \emph{other} cells (as required
under LOCO) mismatches B0006 even more. This observation leads to the central
finding of Section~\ref{sec:central_finding}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{2-mainmatter/chapters/fit_overlay_B0006.png}
    \caption{Closed-form prior fits for the atypical cell B0006. The cell starts
    above rated capacity and regenerates heavily; the fixed closed-form prior
    (route~A) cannot track this without dragging predictions away from the data.}
    \label{fig:fit_b0006}
\end{figure}

Route~B, in contrast, does not use a fixed curve: it learns its Verhulst
parameters during training. On the held-out folds it converged to
$r\approx-0.80$ and a carrying capacity $K\approx0.93$. With a negative rate
constant the logistic law becomes a decay law: for $\mathrm{SOH} < K$ the
learned dynamics give $d\mathrm{SOH}/dt<0$, i.e.\ a monotone fade whose rate
adapts to the training distribution instead of following a fixed shape.
These two learned scalars can be inspected directly, so the interpretability
benefit of the physics prior is retained even though its parameters are learned.

Several observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Every learned model clears the empirical floor.} The pure
    double-exponential curve fit, the physical-floor baseline defined in
    Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}, reaches only $0.0449$ RMSE on
    held-out cells, and the non-recurrent SVR $0.0495$. All learned models beat
    both floors (the heavy Transformer only barely clears the curve fit), so the
    learned models add value beyond curve fitting. The curve fit also transfers
    poorly: applied zero-shot to the $878$-cycle CALCE cell, its error rises to
    $0.67$. Its near-perfect physical consistency ($0.995$) carries little
    information, since a fitted closed-form curve is monotone by construction,
    which is also why it cannot track genuine regeneration.
    \item \textbf{The route-B hybrid is the most accurate model} on unseen NASA
    cells (RMSE $0.0307$), ahead of the LSTM ($0.0331$), the strongest purely
    data-driven baseline; their confidence intervals overlap, so the two are
    statistically tied on accuracy. It is also the most physically consistent
    learned model by a clear margin ($0.939$ vs.\ $0.814$ for the LSTM).
    \item \textbf{Neither Transformer leads on accuracy.} The edge-sized light
    Transformer reaches $0.0395$, and the 13$\times$ larger SOTA-scale model is
    worse, at $0.0428$, with the widest confidence interval of any learned
    model. In this small-data setting, additional attention capacity does not
    improve accuracy; Section~\ref{sec:transformer_gap} examines why.
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset transfer.} The light Transformer attains the
    best zero-shot NASA$\rightarrow$CALCE RMSE ($0.0627$), followed by the
    route-B hybrid ($0.0802$), the heavy Transformer ($0.0817$) and the LSTM
    ($0.0864$); the plain GRU and Neural-ODE degrade to $\approx0.14$. Because
    only one CALCE test cell is available, these numbers are indicative only
    (Section~\ref{sec:transformer_gap}).
    \item \textbf{Route~A (fixed prior) is the weakest hybrid} ($0.0368$). The
    next section explains why.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Central Finding: Learnable Physics (Route B) Outperforms the Fixed Prior}
\label{sec:central_finding}

The most important result of this thesis is a correction of the interim,
single-fold view. On one development fold the fixed double-exponential prior
(route~A) looked best; on the \emph{full} four-fold LOCO evaluation this does not
hold. Table~\ref{tab:perfold} breaks the two hybrid routes --- and the strongest
data-driven baseline, the LSTM --- down per test cell.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Per-cell SOH RMSE (mean over 3 seeds) under leave-one-cell-out. The
fixed prior (route~A) and the purely data-driven LSTM are excellent on the
typical cells (B0007, B0018) but degrade sharply on the atypical B0005/B0006;
the learnable route~B is uniformly robust across all four.}
\label{tab:perfold}
\begin{tabularx}{0.85\textwidth}{@{} l *{4}{>{\centering\arraybackslash}X} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Test cell} & \textbf{B0005} & \textbf{B0006} & \textbf{B0007} & \textbf{B0018} \\
\midrule
Hybrid (route A) & 0.054 & 0.044 & 0.028 & 0.021 \\
Hybrid (route B) & \textbf{0.035} & \textbf{0.037} & \textbf{0.025} & 0.027 \\
LSTM             & 0.044 & 0.046 & \textbf{0.025} & \textbf{0.017} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\textbf{Route~A (fixed closed-form prior) helps typical cells but hurts atypical
ones.} It is strong on B0018 ($0.021$) but worst on B0005 ($0.054$) and B0006
($0.044$). B0006 starts above rated capacity with the heaviest regeneration
(Figure~\ref{fig:fit_b0006}); a prior fit on the other cells simply does not
describe it, so the physics penalty drags predictions the wrong way. The LSTM
shows the same failure pattern ($0.044$/$0.046$ on the atypical cells): both a
rigid prior and a purely data-driven model struggle on cells that differ from the
training population, which is where a fleet BMS is most exposed.
Figure~\ref{fig:soh_b0005} shows this on B0005.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{2-mainmatter/chapters/soh_overlay_B0005.png}
    \caption{SOH trajectory on the held-out cell B0005. Every model --- including
    the fixed-prior route~A --- sits \emph{above} the measured curve, over-estimating
    SOH as the real capacity fades faster than a prior fit on the other cells
    predicts. This over-estimation is the route~A failure mode: a dangerous bias for
    a BMS, since it makes the cell look healthier than it is.}
    \label{fig:soh_b0005}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Route~B (learnable ODE-residual) is consistent across all folds}
($0.025$--$0.037$) and has the best physical consistency (0.939), because it
learns its ODE parameters from each training set instead of importing a fixed and
possibly mismatched curve. For a BMS that must generalise to unseen cells, this
consistency matters more than peak accuracy on one favourable cell.

\paragraph{Summary.} The lightweight physics-informed approach holds, but the
physics mechanism matters: the learnable ODE-residual (route~B) should be
preferred over the fixed closed-form prior (route~A). Route~B costs roughly
$3\times$ more training time (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}) in exchange for
consistency across cells and the best physical plausibility. Since training is
performed offline, this is an acceptable trade for a safety-critical application.

\section{The Transformer Question: Heavy Attention Does Not Pay on Scarce Data}
\label{sec:transformer_gap}

Chapters 1 and 2 motivated this work with the observation that attention models
lead the literature on accuracy but are too heavy for standard BMS hardware, so a
lightweight physics-informed model should aim to match their accuracy at a
fraction of the footprint. The measured results support a stronger statement: at
realistic data volumes, the SOTA-scale Transformer loses its accuracy advantage
as well. Transformer results in the literature (e.g.\ SGEformer) are obtained on
large training corpora, whereas run-to-failure data at that scale is rarely
available for a given cell design. This section quantifies the gap and examines
its cause.

\subsection{The Paired Gap}

Table~\ref{tab:gap} reports the paired comparison against the heavy (SOTA-scale)
Transformer. The test is performed at the fold level ($n=12$ leave-one-cell-out
experiments $=4$ cells $\times$ 3 seeds), which is the appropriate unit of
replication: per-cycle predictions within a cell are strongly autocorrelated, so
pairing thousands of them (as an earlier version of this analysis did) inflates
the effective sample size and yields a meaningless $p\approx5\times10^{-94}$. In
addition, because the models use different sequence lengths, those per-cycle
pairs were not aligned to the same cycles.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Hybrid vs.\ the heavy (SOTA-scale) Transformer, paired at the \emph{fold}
level ($n=12$), with the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.}
\label{tab:gap}
\small
\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\begin{tabular}{l c c c c c c}
\toprule
\textbf{Comparison} & \textbf{Mean} & \textbf{Transf.} & \textbf{Rel.\ gap} & \textbf{Folds won} & \textbf{Wilcoxon $p$} & \textbf{Sig.} \\
\midrule
Route A vs.\ Transf.\ (SOH RMSE) & 0.0368 & 0.0428 & $-14.2\%$ & 6/12 & 0.622 & no \\

\end{table}

On the in-distribution leave-one-cell-out task the route-B hybrid has a lower
mean SOH RMSE than the SOTA-scale Transformer ($-28.3\%$, winning 9 of 12 folds),
but with only four cells the cross-cell variance is high and the difference is
not statistically significant (Wilcoxon $p=0.092$). The physical-consistency
difference, in contrast, is significant: route~B is more physically consistent
than the Transformer on every fold (12/12, $p\approx5\times10^{-4}$). The
defensible claim is therefore that the hybrid is significantly more physically
plausible, with a large but not statistically significant accuracy advantage.
For a safety-critical BMS, the plausibility property is arguably the more
important of the two. Figure~\ref{fig:differential} shows the per-cycle
differential error on B0018: positive values mean the hybrid is closer to the
ground truth, and the hybrid stays ahead over the second half of the cell's life
as it approaches end-of-life.

additional capacity. Two stress tests examine this directly

The conclusion is that on battery-prognostics data of this size, data scarcity
matters more than model capacity. The Transformer accuracy reported in the
literature does not carry over to the small-fleet regime, while the physics
prior, by constraining the hypothesis space, contributes more than the
additional $200$k attention parameters do. This does not contradict the reviewed
literature; it limits its scope: large attention models justify their cost only
when the training corpus is large.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Nuance}

The Transformer family is not beaten everywhere: on the zero-shot
NASA$\rightarrow$CALCE transfer the \emph{light} Transformer has the lowest mean
Cross RMSE ($0.0627$, Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}), with the route-B hybrid the best
of the recurrent family ($0.0802$) ahead of the heavy Transformer ($0.0817$) and
LSTM ($0.0864$), while the plain GRU and Neural-ODE degrade to $\approx0.14$.
This result must be read cautiously: the cross-dataset protocol has only a
\emph{single} CALCE test cell (CS2\_35), so each number is one cell over three
seeds and the ranking among the leaders is not reliable. The dependable signal
is that the physics-regularised hybrid remains competitive out of distribution
while the plain recurrent baselines degrade, which is consistent with the
regularisation interpretation above.

Four conclusions follow:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The de-confound control separates the two ingredients of
    route~B.} Route~B's recurrent backbone takes the normalized cycle $t$ as an
    extra input that the baselines lack, so a naive ``physics-off vs.\ route-B''
    comparison would conflate the ODE loss with that feature. The control shows
    that, on this quick 2-fold protocol, the $t$-channel supplies most of the raw
    accuracy gain ($0.0304 \rightarrow 0.0239$), while the ODE residual on top is
    accuracy-neutral within noise ($0.0253$) but gives the best physical
    consistency of every variant ($0.933$ vs.\ $0.902$). The two roles are
    therefore separable: the extra coordinate improves accuracy, and the physics
    improves plausibility. On the full four-fold benchmark
    (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}) the combination is also the most accurate model
    overall, and plausibility is the axis on which the hybrid's advantage is
    statistically significant, so the physics term contributes where it is
    claimed to.
    \item \textbf{Route~B costs $\sim$3$\times$ the training time} of the
    no-physics baseline ($4.1$\,s vs.\ $1.5$\,s on this protocol). This training
    cost is paid offline; the deployed inference network is unchanged.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty weighting did not have the impact we anticipated
    here.} On this small, clean problem the dynamic homoscedastic weighting did
    not improve on a well-chosen fixed weight ($0.0332$ vs.\ $0.0315$ for the same
    route-A backbone); the learned $\sigma$ tended to down-weight the data term
    more than was helpful. This refines the methodology of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}: the automatic weighting of Section~4.3 is a
    sound mechanism, but on a dataset of this size a carefully chosen fixed
    weight performs equally well (here slightly better), so the fixed weight is
    used for the headline models.
    \item \textbf{Within route~A the double-exponential prior beats the
    logistic} ($0.0315$ vs.\ $0.0364$), consistent with the AIC verdict of
    Table~\ref{tab:prior_fits} --- the two-timescale law is the better fixed
    prior, though neither rescues route~A from its atypical-cell failure mode
    (Section~\ref{sec:central_finding}).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Matched-size validation.} Shrinking the route-B hybrid from the 41k
benchmark size to the deployable 11k costs essentially nothing: evaluated on the
same full four-fold leave-one-cell-out protocol, the matched-size model scores
SOH RMSE $0.0305\pm0.0085$ with physical consistency $0.924$ --- statistically
identical to the $4\times$ larger model ($0.0307$) --- so the ``lightweight''
claim holds at the size actually deployed. Two methodological notes bound this
result. First, as stated in Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}, the int8
sizes are $4\times$ post-training-quantisation estimates and the M4 latency is
analytic, not an on-board measurement. Second, the MAC counter instruments only
the linear and recurrent layers, so the Transformers' attention matrix products
($QK^\top$ and $AV$) are \emph{not} counted --- a bias in the Transformers'
favour, which makes the hybrid's reported latency advantage conservative.

\section{Implementation Caveats}
\label{sec:caveats}

For completeness, four implementation details that bound the claims are recorded
here rather than left implicit:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Life-fraction input.} The route-B backbone receives a
    normalized cycle coordinate whose test-time normalization uses the test
    trajectory's own length --- information not available online. This is a mild
    optimistic bias for route~B; the profiled deployment path runs with a zeroed
    coordinate, and a fixed-scale normalization that aligns the evaluated and
    deployed paths is future work.
    \item \textbf{Quantisation and latency.} Int8 flash figures are $4\times$
    post-training-quantisation estimates of measured fp32 sizes; the M4 latency
    is analytic (and conservative toward the Transformers, whose attention
    matrix products are excluded from the MAC count).
    \item \textbf{RUL persistence.} EOL \emph{labels} use the 5-cycle persistence
    rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}; the RUL curves derived
    from \emph{predicted} SOH trajectories at evaluation time use a 3-cycle
    persistence, a slightly more responsive setting applied identically to every
    model, so PHM comparisons between models are unaffected.
    \item \textbf{PHM metric coverage.} $\alpha$-$\lambda$, PH and CRA are
    computable only where both the true and the predicted trajectory cross the
    EOL threshold; folds where a model never predicts EOL drop out of that
    model's PHM average. This affects all models symmetrically but means the PHM
    columns average over slightly different fold subsets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:results_summary}

The measured evaluation supports the following conclusions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learnable physics (route~B).} The learnable ODE-residual
    performs best overall: it has the lowest mean SOH RMSE of all models
    ($0.0307$), consistent accuracy on every unseen cell ($0.025$--$0.037$), and
    the highest physical consistency ($0.939$; higher than the SOTA Transformer
    on all 12 folds, $p\approx5\times10^{-4}$). Its accuracy advantage over the
    Transformer ($-28.3\%$) is large in mean but not significant at the fold
    level ($p=0.092$, four cells). The fixed closed-form prior (route~A) can
    degrade accuracy on atypical cells and is not recommended.
    \item \textbf{Transformer comparison.} Attention models lead the literature
    on accuracy when trained on large corpora. Under the data scarcity typical
    of this application, the SOTA-scale model overfits (13$\times$ more
    parameters made it worse), so the lightweight hybrid meets the original goal
    of Transformer-class accuracy at a fraction of the footprint. Two caveats
    apply: the LSTM is statistically tied with route~B in-distribution, and the
    light Transformer leads the single-cell cross-dataset transfer.
    \item \textbf{Negative results.} The homoscedastic uncertainty weighting did
    not improve on a well-chosen fixed weight on this dataset, so the fixed
    weight is used for the headline models. The ablation control likewise shows
    that the physics term's distinctive contribution is physical plausibility,
    with a neutral-to-positive effect on accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Deployment.} The matched-size route-B hybrid requires
    $11.7$\,KB of flash and $1.25$\,KB of RAM on the Cortex-M4 budget, $1/20$
    the flash and $1/11$ the latency of the SOTA-scale Transformer, with better
    accuracy, the best calibration among the learned models (CRA $0.30$), and no
    accuracy loss from the size reduction.
\end{itemize}

These outcomes replace the interim report's claim that the hybrid always wins
with a more precise conclusion: a lightweight recurrent model with a learnable
physics regulariser is the best-balanced choice for deployable battery
prognostics, and at realistic data volumes a heavyweight attention model is
neither small enough for the edge nor more accurate.
