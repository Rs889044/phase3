\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the final, \emph{measured} evaluation of the proposed
framework. Every number below is produced by the reproducible pipeline
(data $\rightarrow$ features $\rightarrow$ train $\rightarrow$ eval $\rightarrow$
deploy) on the cleaned datasets; none are illustrative placeholders. Where the
measured outcome contradicts the earlier interim narrative, the contradiction is
surfaced and explained rather than hidden, in keeping with the honesty
requirements of a prognostics study.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}

All accuracy results use the protocol defined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation} (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_strategy}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NASA leave-one-cell-out (LOCO):} all four folds. In each fold
    one cell is held out for \emph{test} and a second cell is held out for
    \emph{validation} (early stopping and model selection); the model is trained
    on the remaining two cells. Each fold is repeated over \textbf{3 random
    seeds}. Scalers and physics priors are fit on the \emph{training cells only}
    to prevent leakage.
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset generalization:} train on the full NASA set,
    evaluate zero-shot on CALCE CS2\_35. As established in
    Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}, both datasets are LiCoO$_2$
    (LCO); this is therefore a \emph{cross-dataset / cross-load /
    cross-form-factor} test, not a cross-chemistry one.
    \item \textbf{Reporting:} SOH RMSE is reported as the mean over folds$\times$seeds
    with a 95\% bootstrap confidence interval (1000 resamples); model comparisons
    are tested with the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.
\end{itemize}

Seven learned architectures are compared: \textbf{two Transformers} --- a
\emph{light}, edge-sized model (17k parameters, hyperparameter-tuned like every
other baseline) and a \emph{heavy}, SOTA-scale model (225k parameters, the
state-of-the-art accuracy reference representing the attention models reported in
the literature) --- an LSTM, a vanilla GRU, a genuine PIML baseline (Neural-ODE),
and the proposed hybrid in its two physics variants: \textbf{route~A} (fixed
closed-form Verhulst/double-exponential prior used as a soft penalty) and
\textbf{route~B} (a \emph{learnable} ODE-residual whose parameters are trained
jointly with the network). They are measured against the two non-learned
reference floors of Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}: the empirical
double-exponential curve fit and a non-recurrent SVR.

\section{Physics Fits and Interpretability}
\label{sec:physics_interpretability}

Before training, the empirical degradation priors were fit to the per-cell SOH
trajectories (training cells only). Table~\ref{tab:prior_fits} reports the
\emph{real} fitted parameters --- replacing the fabricated coefficients
($a{=}0.0644$, $K{=}1.0000$) that appeared in the interim report. Two facts follow
directly from these fits and shape the rest of the chapter.

$\mathrm{SOH}=Q/Q_\mathrm{rated}$ convention.}
\label{tab:prior_fits}
\small
\begin{tabular}{l c c c c}
\toprule
\textbf{Cell} & \textbf{Logistic $K$} & \textbf{Logistic $R^2$} & \textbf{Double-exp $R^2$} & \textbf{$\Delta$AIC (d$-$l)} \\
\midrule

\bottomrule

\textbf{(1) The two-timescale prior is justified.} The double-exponential model
achieves a lower AIC than the logistic on \emph{every} cell ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$
from $-86$ to $-347$, always favouring double-exp), with a higher $R^2$ and a
smaller residual spread on each cell (Figure~\ref{fig:redundancy}). This
empirically settles the Verhulst-vs-double-exponential redundancy question: the
two-timescale decay (fast SEI stabilisation + slow active-material loss) is a
strictly better closed-form description than the single logistic S-curve, so the
hybrid carries both priors and selects between them by configuration.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{2-mainmatter/chapters/redundancy.png}
    \caption{Closed-form prior comparison per cell. (Left) the double-exponential
    attains a higher fit quality ($R^2$) than the logistic on every cell; (Right)
    it also produces a smaller residual spread. This justifies retaining the
    two-timescale prior alongside the logistic.}
    \label{fig:redundancy}
\end{figure}

\textbf{(2) A fixed prior cannot describe atypical cells.} Cell B0006 begins
\emph{above} rated capacity (initial SOH $\approx101.8\%$) and exhibits the
heaviest capacity regeneration. Figure~\ref{fig:fit_b0006} shows that even the
flexible double-exponential, when fit to B0006, struggles with its early
super-rated bulge --- and a prior fit on the \emph{other} cells (as required
under LOCO) mismatches B0006 even more. This is the seed of the central finding in
Section~\ref{sec:central_finding}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{2-mainmatter/chapters/fit_overlay_B0006.png}
    \caption{Closed-form prior fits for the atypical cell B0006. The cell starts
    above rated capacity and regenerates heavily; the fixed closed-form prior
    (route~A) cannot track this without dragging predictions away from the data.}
    \label{fig:fit_b0006}
\end{figure}

In contrast, \textbf{route~B does not import a fixed curve}: it learns its Verhulst
parameters during training. On the held-out folds it converged to
$r\approx-0.80$ and a carrying capacity $K\approx0.93$. With a negative rate
constant the logistic law inverts into a decay law: for $\mathrm{SOH} < K$ the
learned dynamics give $d\mathrm{SOH}/dt<0$, i.e.\ monotone fade whose rate
adapts to the training distribution rather than forcing a mismatched fixed shape.
These two learned scalars remain directly inspectable --- the interpretability
benefit of the physics prior survives the move from fixed to learnable parameters.

The honest reading of Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} is more nuanced than ``the hybrid
always wins'':

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Every learned model clears the empirical floor.} The pure
    double-exponential curve fit --- the ``physical floor'' go/no-go test set up in
    Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation} --- reaches only $0.0449$ RMSE on
    held-out cells, and the non-recurrent SVR $0.0495$. All learned models beat
    both floors (the heavy Transformer only barely clears the curve fit), so the
    learning machinery adds genuine value over curve-fitting rather than merely
    reproducing it. The pure curve fit is also fundamentally non-transferable:
    extrapolated zero-shot to CALCE's $878$-cycle life it collapses (Cross RMSE
    $0.67$), and its near-perfect physical consistency ($0.995$) is trivial ---
    the fitted curve is monotone by construction, which is exactly why it cannot
    track real regeneration.
    \item \textbf{The route-B hybrid is the most accurate model} on unseen NASA
    cells (RMSE $0.0307$), ahead of the LSTM ($0.0331$), the strongest purely
    data-driven baseline; their confidence intervals overlap, so the two are
    statistically tied on accuracy. It is simultaneously \textbf{the most
    physically consistent} learned model by a wide margin ($0.939$ vs.\ $0.814$
    for the LSTM).
    \item \textbf{Neither Transformer leads on accuracy.} The edge-sized light
    Transformer reaches $0.0395$ and the 13$\times$ larger SOTA-scale model is
    \emph{worse}, at $0.0428$ with the widest confidence interval of any learned
    model. On this small-data, leave-one-cell-out regime, adding attention
    capacity hurts rather than helps --- Section~\ref{sec:transformer_gap}
    quantifies why.
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset transfer favours attention and physics.} The
    light Transformer attains the best zero-shot NASA$\rightarrow$CALCE RMSE
    ($0.0627$), with the route-B hybrid next among learned models ($0.0802$,
    at a quarter of the light Transformer's deployed size), then the heavy
    Transformer ($0.0817$) and LSTM ($0.0864$); the plain GRU and Neural-ODE
    degrade markedly ($\approx0.14$). This single-cell transfer must be read
    cautiously (Section~\ref{sec:transformer_gap}).
    \item \textbf{Route~A (fixed prior) is the weakest hybrid} ($0.0368$). The
    next section explains why.
\end{itemize}

\section{The Central Finding: Learnable Physics (Route B) is the Robust Winner}
\label{sec:central_finding}

The most important result of this thesis is a correction of the interim,
single-fold view. On one development fold the fixed double-exponential prior
(route~A) looked best; on the \emph{full} four-fold LOCO evaluation this does not
hold. Table~\ref{tab:perfold} breaks the two hybrid routes --- and the strongest
data-driven baseline, the LSTM --- down per test cell.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Per-cell SOH RMSE (mean over 3 seeds) under leave-one-cell-out. The
fixed prior (route~A) and the purely data-driven LSTM are excellent on the
typical cells (B0007, B0018) but degrade sharply on the atypical B0005/B0006;
the learnable route~B is uniformly robust across all four.}
\label{tab:perfold}
\begin{tabularx}{0.85\textwidth}{@{} l *{4}{>{\centering\arraybackslash}X} @{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Test cell} & \textbf{B0005} & \textbf{B0006} & \textbf{B0007} & \textbf{B0018} \\
\midrule
Hybrid (route A) & 0.054 & 0.044 & 0.028 & 0.021 \\
Hybrid (route B) & \textbf{0.035} & \textbf{0.037} & \textbf{0.025} & 0.027 \\
LSTM             & 0.044 & 0.046 & \textbf{0.025} & \textbf{0.017} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

\textbf{Route~A (fixed closed-form prior) helps typical cells but hurts atypical
ones.} It is strong on B0018 ($0.021$) but worst on B0005 ($0.054$) and B0006
($0.044$). B0006 starts above rated capacity with the heaviest regeneration
(Figure~\ref{fig:fit_b0006}); a prior fit on the other cells simply does not
describe it, so the physics penalty drags predictions the wrong way. Notably, the
LSTM shows the \emph{same} failure pattern ($0.044$/$0.046$ on the atypical
cells): without a physics mechanism that adapts, both a rigid prior and pure
data-driving struggle exactly where a fleet BMS is most exposed --- on cells
unlike the training population. Figure~\ref{fig:soh_b0005} shows this concretely
on B0005.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{2-mainmatter/chapters/soh_overlay_B0005.png}
    \caption{SOH trajectory on the held-out cell B0005. Every model --- including
    the fixed-prior route~A --- sits \emph{above} the measured curve, over-estimating
    SOH as the real capacity fades faster than a prior fit on the other cells
    predicts. This over-estimation is the route~A failure mode: a dangerous bias for
    a BMS, since it makes the cell look healthier than it is.}
    \label{fig:soh_b0005}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Route~B (learnable ODE-residual) is the robust winner.} It is consistent
on \emph{every} fold ($0.025$--$0.037$) and has the best physical consistency
(0.939), precisely because it \emph{learns} its ODE parameters per training set
rather than importing a fixed, possibly mismatched curve. For a BMS that must
generalise to cells it has never seen, this robustness --- not peak accuracy on
one favourable cell --- is the property that matters.

\paragraph{Takeaway.} The ``lightweight physics-informed model'' claim holds, but
the \emph{right} physics mechanism is the \textbf{learnable ODE-residual
(route~B)}, not the fixed closed-form prior (route~A). Route~B trades roughly
$3\times$ training cost (Section~\ref{sec:ablations}) for robustness across cells
and the best physical plausibility --- exactly what a safety-critical application
needs.

\section{The Transformer Question: Heavy Attention Does Not Pay on Scarce Data}
\label{sec:transformer_gap}

The motivating narrative of this thesis (Chapters 1 and 2) was
that attention models are the state of the art in accuracy but are too heavy for
the BMS edge, so a lightweight physics-informed model should aim to \emph{match}
their accuracy at a fraction of the footprint. The measured evaluation supports a
\emph{stronger} statement: under realistic per-fleet data volumes, the SOTA-scale
Transformer does not even hold up its accuracy end of that bargain. Reported
Transformer results in the literature (e.g.\ SGEformer) are obtained on large
training corpora; a BMS vendor rarely has run-to-failure data at that scale for
each cell design. This section quantifies the gap and diagnoses its cause.

\subsection{The Paired Gap}

Table~\ref{tab:gap} reports the paired comparison against the heavy (SOTA-scale)
Transformer. We test at the \emph{fold} level ($n=12$ leave-one-cell-out
experiments $=4$ cells $\times$ 3 seeds), the honest unit of replication:
per-cycle predictions within a cell are strongly autocorrelated, so pairing
thousands of them (as an earlier version did) pseudoreplicates and yields a
spuriously tiny $p\approx5\times10^{-94}$ --- and, because the models use
different sequence lengths, those ``paired'' residuals were also cycle-misaligned.

level ($n=12$), with the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test.}
\label{tab:gap}

\begin{tabular}{l c c c c c c}
\toprule
\textbf{Comparison} & \textbf{Mean} & \textbf{Transf.} & \textbf{Rel.\ gap} & \textbf{Folds won} & \textbf{Wilcoxon $p$} & \textbf{Sig.} \\

On the in-distribution leave-one-cell-out task the route-B hybrid has a
\emph{large lower mean} SOH RMSE than the SOTA-scale Transformer ($-28.3\%$,
winning 9 of 12 folds), but with only four cells the cross-cell variance is high
and the difference is \emph{not} statistically significant (Wilcoxon $p=0.092$).
What \emph{is} robustly significant is physical consistency: route~B is more
physically consistent than the Transformer on \emph{every} fold (12/12,
$p\approx5\times10^{-4}$). The honest claim is therefore ``significantly more
physically plausible, with a large but not-significant accuracy edge'' --- exactly
the property a safety-critical BMS values. Figure~\ref{fig:differential}
shows the per-cycle differential error on B0018: positive values mean the hybrid
beats the baseline, and the hybrid is ahead over the second (more safety-critical)
half of the cell's life as it approaches end-of-life.

confidence interval --- the signature of a model whose capacity cannot be
exploited by the available data. Two stress tests make the mechanism explicit
(Figure~\ref{fig:scarcity}).

The conclusion for the thesis narrative is direct: on battery-prognostics data of
realistic size, \textbf{data scarcity dominates model capacity}. The literature's
Transformer accuracy does not transfer to the small-fleet regime; the
physics prior, by constraining the hypothesis space, is worth more than
$200$k extra attention parameters. This does not contradict the reviewed
literature --- it bounds it: attention models earn their footprint only where
training corpora are large.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Nuance}

The Transformer family is not beaten everywhere: on the zero-shot
NASA$\rightarrow$CALCE transfer the \emph{light} Transformer has the lowest mean
Cross RMSE ($0.0627$, Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}), with the route-B hybrid the best
of the recurrent family ($0.0802$) ahead of the heavy Transformer ($0.0817$) and
LSTM ($0.0864$), while the plain GRU and Neural-ODE degrade to $\approx0.14$.
This result must be read cautiously: the cross-dataset protocol has only a
\emph{single} CALCE test cell (CS2\_35), so each number is one cell over three
seeds and the ranking among the leaders is not reliable. The robust signal is
that the physics-regularised hybrid remains competitive out of distribution
where the plain recurrent baselines fall away --- consistent with the
regularisation interpretation above.

\section{Ablation Studies}
\label{sec:ablations}

To isolate the contribution of each design choice, three ablations were run. The
design ablation (route / prior / weighting) uses 2 folds $\times$ 2 seeds for
speed; trends are consistent with the main benchmark, but absolute numbers are
indicative.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Physics-design ablation (route, prior, weighting), with a de-confound
control. ``Physics OFF $+$ t-channel'' has the route-B architecture (and thus the
extra cycle-coordinate input) but no ODE-residual penalty, isolating the input
feature from the physics.}
\label{tab:ablation}
\begin{tabular}{l c c c}
\toprule
\textbf{Variant} & \textbf{SOH RMSE} & \textbf{Phys.Cons.} & \textbf{Train (s)} \\
\midrule
Physics OFF $+$ t-channel (control)    & \textbf{0.0239} & 0.902 & 4.87 \\
Route B $\cdot$ ODE-residual (t $+$ physics) & 0.0253 & \textbf{0.933} & 4.09 \\
Physics OFF (no t, no physics)         & 0.0304 & 0.896 & 1.52 \\
Route A $\cdot$ double-exp             & 0.0315 & 0.903 & 2.01 \\
Route A $\cdot$ uncertainty-wt         & 0.0332 & 0.895 & 1.24 \\
Route A $\cdot$ logistic               & 0.0364 & 0.884 & 1.52 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Four conclusions follow:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{The de-confound control separates the two ingredients of
    route~B.} Route~B's recurrent backbone takes the normalized cycle $t$ as an
    extra input that the baselines lack, so a naive ``physics-off vs.\ route-B''
    comparison would conflate the ODE loss with that feature. The control shows
    that, on this quick 2-fold protocol, the $t$-channel supplies most of the raw
    accuracy gain ($0.0304 \rightarrow 0.0239$), while the ODE residual on top is
    accuracy-neutral within noise ($0.0253$) but delivers the \emph{best physical
    consistency of every variant} ($0.933$ vs.\ $0.902$). The division of labour
    is therefore explicit: \emph{the extra coordinate buys accuracy; the physics
    buys plausibility} --- and on the full four-fold benchmark
    (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}) the combination is also the most accurate model
    overall. This is precisely the axis (plausibility) on which the hybrid's
    advantage is statistically significant, so the physics term is earning its
    place where it is claimed to.
    \item \textbf{Route~B costs $\sim$3$\times$ the training time} of the
    no-physics baseline ($4.1$\,s vs.\ $1.5$\,s on this protocol) --- the
    accuracy/compute trade-off quantified explicitly. Training cost is paid
    offline; the deployed inference network is unchanged.
    \item \textbf{Uncertainty weighting did not have the impact we anticipated
    here.} On this small, clean problem the dynamic homoscedastic weighting did
    not improve on a well-chosen fixed weight ($0.0332$ vs.\ $0.0315$ for the same
    route-A backbone), the learned $\sigma$ tending to down-weight the data term
    more than helped. We report this transparently: it gently refines the
    methodology of Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} --- the automatic weighting of
    Section~4.3 is a sound and convenient mechanism, but it is not free, and on a
    dataset of this size a carefully chosen fixed weight is an equally good (here,
    slightly better) default. We therefore adopt the fixed weight for the headline
    models.
    \item \textbf{Within route~A the double-exponential prior beats the
    logistic} ($0.0315$ vs.\ $0.0364$), consistent with the AIC verdict of
    Table~\ref{tab:prior_fits} --- the two-timescale law is the better fixed
    prior, though neither rescues route~A from its atypical-cell failure mode
    (Section~\ref{sec:central_finding}).
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Matched-size validation.} Shrinking the route-B hybrid from the 41k
benchmark size to the deployable 11k costs essentially nothing: evaluated on the
same full four-fold leave-one-cell-out protocol, the matched-size model scores
SOH RMSE $0.0305\pm0.0085$ with physical consistency $0.924$ --- statistically
identical to the $4\times$ larger model ($0.0307$) --- so the ``lightweight''
claim is earned at the size actually deployed, by measurement rather than
assertion. (As noted in Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}, the deployment
stack is \textbf{PyTorch + ONNX} rather than TensorFlow Lite Micro, and the M4
latency is an analytic estimate, not an on-board measurement.)

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:results_summary}

The measured evaluation supports a refined, more defensible version of the thesis
hypothesis:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physics helps, and the mechanism matters.} The
    \emph{learnable} ODE-residual (route~B) is the robust winner: the lowest mean
    SOH RMSE of all models ($0.0307$), uniform across every unseen cell
    ($0.025$--$0.037$), and \emph{significantly} the most physically consistent
    model ($0.939$; higher than the SOTA Transformer on all 12 folds,
    $p\approx5\times10^{-4}$). Its accuracy edge over the Transformer ($-28.3\%$)
    is large in mean but not significant at the fold level ($p=0.092$, four
    cells). The \emph{fixed} closed-form prior (route~A) can actively hurt
    atypical cells and is \emph{not} recommended.
    \item \textbf{The Transformer story is sharpened, not overturned.} Attention
    models are the literature's accuracy state of the art \emph{on large corpora};
    under realistic per-fleet data scarcity the SOTA-scale model overfits ---
    13$\times$ more parameters made it \emph{worse} --- so the lightweight hybrid
    matches the original goal (``Transformer-class accuracy at a fraction of the
    footprint'') with margin to spare. The honest caveats: the LSTM is
    statistically tied with route~B in-distribution, and the light Transformer
    leads the (single-cell, low-confidence) cross-dataset transfer.
    \item \textbf{Reported transparently.} The dynamic homoscedastic uncertainty
    weighting did not have the impact we expected on this small, clean problem ---
    a well-chosen fixed weight did as well or slightly better --- so the fixed
    weight is used for the headline models. Likewise, the ablation control shows
    the physics term's unique, significant contribution is physical plausibility
    (with accuracy neutral-to-positive), not a magic accuracy ingredient.
    \item \textbf{Lightweight by measurement.} The matched-size route-B hybrid is
    $11.7$\,KB / $1.25$\,KB int8 on the Cortex-M4 budget --- $1/20$ the flash and
    $1/11$ the latency of the SOTA-scale Transformer --- with \emph{better}
    accuracy and calibration (best learned CRA, $0.30$) and no accuracy loss from
    the size reduction.
\end{itemize}

These outcomes correct the interim report's blanket ``the hybrid always wins''
narrative into a sharper engineering conclusion: \emph{a lightweight, learnable
physics-regularised recurrent model is the best-balanced choice for trustworthy,
deployable battery prognostics --- and on realistic data volumes, heavyweight
attention is not merely too big for the edge, it is also not more accurate}.
