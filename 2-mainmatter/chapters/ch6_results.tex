\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{ch:results}

This chapter reports the final evaluation of the proposed framework. All numbers
are produced by the reproducible pipeline (data preparation, feature extraction,
training, evaluation, deployment profiling) on the cleaned datasets; the pipeline
seeds every model immediately before construction, so each reported result is
independent of what else runs in the notebook. Where a measured outcome differs
from the interim report, the difference is stated and explained.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
\label{sec:eval_protocol}

All accuracy results use the protocol defined in
Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation} (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation_strategy}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{NASA leave-one-cell-out (LOCO):} all four folds. In each fold
    one cell is held out for \emph{test} and a second cell is held out for
    \emph{validation} (early stopping and model selection); the model is trained
    on the remaining two cells. Each fold is repeated over \textbf{3 random
    seeds}. Scalers and physics priors are fit on the \emph{training cells only}
    to prevent leakage.
    \item \textbf{Symmetric time input:} every learned model receives the same
    fixed-scale normalized cycle coordinate ($\mathrm{cycle}/500$) as an extra
    input channel. A BMS knows the elapsed cycle count online, and the fixed
    scale carries no information about a trajectory's total recorded length, so
    this input is leak-free and no model is privileged with time information.
    (An earlier draft normalized this coordinate per trajectory, which leaked
    the recorded life of the test cell; the flaw was identified and corrected,
    and every number in this chapter uses the corrected protocol.)
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset generalization:} train on the full NASA set,
    evaluate zero-shot on CALCE CS2\_35. As established in
    Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}, both datasets are LiCoO$_2$
    (LCO); this is therefore a \emph{cross-dataset / cross-load /
    cross-form-factor} test, not a cross-chemistry one.
    \item \textbf{Reporting:} SOH RMSE is reported as the mean over folds$\times$seeds
    with a 95\% normal-approximation confidence interval ($1.96\times$ the
    standard error over the 12 runs); model comparisons use the paired Wilcoxon
    signed-rank test, interpreted with the caution discussed in
    Section~\ref{sec:transformer_gap} (seeds within a fold share the test cell,
    so the 12 runs are not fully independent).
\end{itemize}

Eight learned architectures are compared: \textbf{two Transformers} --- a
\emph{light}, edge-sized model (17k parameters, hyperparameter-tuned like every
other baseline) and a \emph{heavy}, high-capacity model (225k parameters, an
accuracy reference at the parameter scale of the attention models reported in
the literature) --- an LSTM, a vanilla GRU, a continuous-time baseline
(Neural-ODE; implemented as a \emph{last-observation} latent-ODE head that
encodes the window's most recent cycle and integrates latent dynamics over a
fixed pseudo-time, so it does not exploit the window history the recurrent
models see), a \textbf{matched-capacity control} (the hybrids' identical 41k
backbone and time input with the physics loss switched off), and the proposed
hybrid in its two physics variants: \textbf{route~A} (fixed closed-form
Verhulst/double-exponential prior used as a soft penalty) and \textbf{route~B}
(a \emph{learnable} ODE-residual whose parameters are trained jointly with the
network). They are measured against the two non-neural reference floors of
Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}: the empirical double-exponential curve
fit and a non-recurrent SVR. The SVR is itself a learned model; ``non-neural''
distinguishes these floors from the deep networks rather than implying they are
free of fitting.

\section{Physics Fits and Interpretability}
\label{sec:physics_interpretability}

trajectories (training cells only). Table~\ref{tab:prior_fits} reports the
fitted parameters, which replace the placeholder coefficients
($a{=}0.0644$, $K{=}1.0000$) of the interim report. Two observations from these
fits are used later in the chapter.

\textbf{(1) The double-exponential prior earns its extra parameter.} The double-exponential model
achieves a lower AIC than the logistic on \emph{every} cell ($\Delta\mathrm{AIC}$
from $-86$ to $-347$, always favouring double-exp), with a higher $R^2$ and a
smaller residual spread on each cell (Figure~\ref{fig:redundancy}). This answers
the redundancy question between the two priors: the more flexible
double-exponential describes the data better than the single logistic S-curve,
so it is retained as the primary closed-form prior and the logistic as an
alternative configuration. One caution accompanies this reading: the
unconstrained fits do not always assign the mechanistically expected signs ---
on B0005 the fast-term amplitude is negative, on B0006 the second exponent is
positive (a growing term), and on two cells an exponent rests at the optimizer
bound --- so the AIC preference is evidence that the four-parameter
\emph{shape} fits better, not a confirmation of the SEI/LAM decomposition
itself (the caveat stated in Chapter~\ref{ch:problem},
Section~\ref{sec:physics_formulation}).

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{2-mainmatter/chapters/redundancy.png}
    \caption{Closed-form prior comparison per cell. (Left) the double-exponential
    attains a higher fit quality ($R^2$) than the logistic on every cell; (Right)
    it also produces a smaller residual spread. This justifies retaining the
    two-timescale prior alongside the logistic.}
    \label{fig:redundancy}
\end{figure}

\textbf{(2) A fixed prior cannot describe atypical cells.} Cell B0006 begins
\emph{above} rated capacity (initial SOH $\approx101.8\%$) and exhibits the
heaviest capacity regeneration. Figure~\ref{fig:fit_b0006} shows that even the
flexible double-exponential, when fit to B0006, struggles with its early
super-rated bulge --- and a prior fit on the \emph{other} cells (as required
under LOCO) mismatches B0006 even more. This observation leads to the central
finding of Section~\ref{sec:central_finding}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{2-mainmatter/chapters/fit_overlay_B0006.png}
    \caption{Closed-form prior fits for the atypical cell B0006. The cell starts
    above rated capacity and regenerates heavily; the fixed closed-form prior
    (route~A) cannot track this without dragging predictions away from the data.}
    \label{fig:fit_b0006}
\end{figure}

Route~B, in contrast, does not use a fixed curve: it learns its Verhulst
parameters during training. On the held-out folds it converged to
$r\approx-0.89$ and a carrying capacity $K\approx0.87$ (both expressed in the
fixed-scale cycle coordinate, so $r$ is a rate per 500 cycles rather than per
cycle, and its physical interpretation is correspondingly coarser; the values
are taken from the best-validation checkpoint, saved together with the network
weights). With a negative rate constant the logistic law becomes a decay law:
for $\mathrm{SOH} < K$ the learned dynamics give $d\mathrm{SOH}/dt<0$, i.e.\ a
monotone fade whose rate adapts to the training distribution instead of
following a fixed shape. One boundary of this reading is stated for
completeness: for SOH \emph{above} the learned $K$ --- which includes much of
the observed early life, since the NASA cells start at SOH $0.93$--$1.02$ ---
the same law yields a small positive drift (about $2\times10^{-4}$ SOH per
cycle at SOH $=1.0$), so strictly the learned prior exerts a weak flattening
pressure early in life and a decay pressure once SOH falls below $K$. Because
the residual is a soft penalty this nuance has little practical effect on the
trained model, but the monotone-fade interpretation holds exactly only below
$K$. These two learned scalars can be inspected directly, so
the interpretability benefit of the physics prior is retained even though its
parameters are learned.

Several observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The matched-capacity control isolates the physics
    contribution.} The control shares route~B's architecture, capacity (41,473
    parameters) and time input, and differs only in the physics loss. It scores
    $0.0377$; adding the learnable ODE residual moves the same network to
    $0.0292$ (a $22\%$ reduction in mean RMSE) with a small physical-consistency
    gain ($0.879 \rightarrow 0.884$). The accuracy improvement holds on 11 of
    the 12 paired fold$\times$seed runs, and per cell on three of four with the
    fourth a near-tie (Section~\ref{sec:transformer_gap}, Table~\ref{tab:gap}).
    The main accuracy gain is therefore attributable to the physics term, not
    to the hybrid's larger capacity relative to the small baselines.
    \item \textbf{The route-B hybrid has the lowest mean SOH RMSE} ($0.0292 \pm
    0.0100$) and the highest physical consistency among learned models
    ($0.884$). The confidence intervals of the closest competitors overlap, so
    the accuracy lead is descriptive (see Section~\ref{sec:transformer_gap} for
    the paired test).
    \item \textbf{Every learned model clears the non-neural floors} (double-exp
    $0.0449$, SVR $0.0492$). The curve fit also transfers poorly: applied
    zero-shot to the $878$-cycle CALCE cell its error rises to $0.67$, and its
    near-perfect physical consistency ($0.995$) carries little information,
    since a fitted closed-form curve is monotone by construction.
    \item \textbf{Attention capacity buys no reliable return.} The heavy
    Transformer's mean is marginally lower than the light one's ($0.0369$ vs.\
    $0.0379$) with overlapping intervals --- a $13\times$ parameter and
    $8.7\times$ int8-flash increase for a difference well inside the noise, and
    lower physical consistency ($0.770$ vs.\ $0.789$).
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset transfer is led by the fixed-prior hybrid and
    the LSTM} (route~A $0.0892$, LSTM $0.0925$, control $0.0998$).
    \textbf{Route~B degrades to $0.2077$} out of distribution, a negative
    result analysed in
    Section~\ref{sec:cross_dataset}: its learned dynamics depend on the cycle
    coordinate, which extrapolates far outside the training range on the
    long-lived CALCE cell. Only one CALCE test cell is available, so all
    cross-dataset numbers are indicative.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
\textbf{Effect of the shared time input on the baselines.} Supplying the cycle
coordinate to every model is a design choice. Comparisons against the
pre-correction run (in which the baselines received no time input, since the
leaked coordinate went only to the hybrids) indicate a mixed effect on the
small baselines, and the matched-capacity control shows the coordinate is not
by itself responsible for the hybrid's accuracy: the control has the same input
and does not approach route~B. The benchmark conclusion therefore does not rest
on this design choice.

\section{The Central Finding: Learnable Physics (Route B) Outperforms the Fixed Prior}
\label{sec:central_finding}

The most important comparison in this thesis is between the two physics
mechanisms, with the strongest purely data-driven recurrent baseline (the LSTM)
alongside. Table~\ref{tab:perfold} breaks the result down per test cell.

Three observations follow. First, \textbf{route~B is better than or equal to
route~A on all four cells}, with the largest margins on B0006 (the cell that
starts above rated capacity with the heaviest regeneration, exactly where a
prior fit on other cells does not apply; Figure~\ref{fig:fit_b0006}) and B0007;
on B0018 the LSTM is the best single model ($0.020$), a reminder that no one
architecture dominates every cell.
Second, \textbf{the purely data-driven LSTM fails where the physics helps
most}: its error more than doubles on B0006 ($0.063$). Third, \textbf{B0005 is
difficult for every model} ($0.041$--$0.053$): all of them over-estimate its health, as
Figure~\ref{fig:soh_b0005} shows, because the cell fades faster than the
training population suggests --- over-estimation being the more serious error
direction for a BMS. No model, physics-informed or otherwise, escapes this
population-shift limitation with only two training cells.

\paragraph{Summary.} The lightweight physics-informed approach holds, and the
physics mechanism matters: the learnable ODE-residual (route~B) should be
preferred over the fixed closed-form prior (route~A). Route~B costs roughly
$2$--$3\times$ more training time than the no-physics backbone
(Section~\ref{sec:ablations}) in exchange for accuracy and the best physical
plausibility at identical capacity. Since training is performed offline, this is
an acceptable trade for a safety-critical application.

\section{Comparison with the Transformer Baselines under Data Scarcity}
\label{sec:transformer_gap}

Chapters 1 and 2 motivated this work with the observation that attention models
lead the literature on accuracy but are too heavy for standard BMS hardware, so a
lightweight physics-informed model should aim to match their accuracy at a
fraction of the footprint. The measured results support that position in a
specific form: at this data scale, attention capacity yields no reliable
accuracy return, while its footprint cost is large and certain. Transformer
results in the literature (e.g.\ SGEformer) are obtained on large training
corpora, whereas run-to-failure data at that scale is rarely available for a
given cell design.

\subsection{The Paired Gap}

Table~\ref{tab:gap} reports the paired comparison against the heavy
Transformer and, for the central attribution claim, against the
matched-capacity control. The tests are performed at the fold level ($n=12$
leave-one-cell-out experiments $=4$ cells $\times$ 3 seeds), which is the
appropriate unit of replication: per-cycle predictions within a cell are strongly autocorrelated, so
pairing thousands of them (as an earlier version of this analysis did) inflates
the effective sample size and yields a meaningless $p\approx5\times10^{-94}$. In
addition, because the models use different sequence lengths, those per-cycle
pairs were not aligned to the same cycles.

On the in-distribution leave-one-cell-out task the route-B hybrid has a lower
mean SOH RMSE than the heavy Transformer ($-21.0\%$), but the mean difference
is carried by the Transformer's much larger run-to-run variance rather than by
uniform wins: the hybrid wins only 5 of the 12 individual runs ($p=0.850$),
while the Transformer's worst runs are far worse than the hybrid's worst. The
advantage is therefore one of robustness (a tighter error distribution at a
much lower mean) rather than of run-by-run dominance, and it is not
statistically significant at the run level, let alone at the cell level, where
four independent cells cap the smallest attainable two-sided Wilcoxon $p$ at
$0.125$. The physical-consistency
difference is uniform: route~B is more physically consistent on all 12 runs and
on all 4 cells after seed-averaging (run-level $p\approx5\times10^{-4}$, subject
to the same independence caution). The defensible statement is descriptive: the
hybrid is more physically plausible on every run, with a sizeable mean accuracy
advantage that this dataset cannot confirm statistically.

The comparison against the \textbf{matched-capacity control} (final rows of
Table~\ref{tab:gap}) carries the attribution claim of
Section~\ref{sec:central_finding} and is more uniform than the Transformer
comparison: adding the physics residual to the identical backbone lowers SOH
RMSE on 11 of 12 paired runs (run-level Wilcoxon $p=0.021$, bootstrap 95\%
interval on the gap $[-0.0154,-0.0013]$, excluding zero) and on three of the
four cells after seed-averaging (per-cell means $0.051/0.027/0.012/0.027$ vs.\
$0.059/0.046/0.020/0.027$; the fourth, B0018, is a near-tie at
$0.0273$ vs.\ $0.0265$). The physical-consistency difference against the
control is small ($+0.7\%$, 7 of 12 runs, $p=0.168$): the control already
carries the same backbone and time channel, so the consistency advantage of
the hybrid over the \emph{other} baselines comes partly from that architecture
and partly from the physics term. The same independence caution applies
(seeds within a fold share the test cell, and four cells cap the cell-level
$p$ at $0.125$), so this too is reported descriptively --- but the accuracy
direction is consistent across nearly every paired run, which the Transformer
accuracy gap is not.

computed after aligning the two models' predictions on the same cycle indices.

\subsection{Capacity, Scarcity and Overfitting}
\label{sec:overfitting}

Scaling the Transformer from 17k to 225k parameters (13$\times$) changes its
held-out accuracy from $0.0379$ to $0.0369$ --- a difference well inside the
overlapping confidence intervals --- while lowering its physical consistency
($0.789 \rightarrow 0.770$) and multiplying its int8 footprint by $8.7\times$
(Section~\ref{sec:footprint}). Two stress tests probe the data-volume question
further (Figure~\ref{fig:scarcity}): a training-fraction sweep (10--100\%) and a
train--test generalization-gap comparison at full data. Both run on a reduced
protocol (2 folds $\times$ 2 seeds) and are \textbf{noise-dominated at this
scale}: no model shows a monotonic trend that is stable across runs, and the gap
estimates are small relative to their variability. These sweeps are therefore
reported as indicative rather than as evidence; the dependable, full-protocol
finding is the one above --- large attention capacity produces no reliable
accuracy return at this data scale, and its cost is paid with certainty in
footprint. One scoping note bounds this conclusion: the heavy reference is a
hand-configured, regularized encoder (dropout, weight decay, longer schedule)
rather than a hyperparameter-tuned one --- with four cells a proper search at
that capacity is not feasible --- so the claim is about this representative
225k configuration, not about every conceivable attention model at that
scale.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Transfer}
\label{sec:cross_dataset}

On the zero-shot NASA$\rightarrow$CALCE transfer the fixed-prior hybrid leads
(route~A $0.0892$), followed by the LSTM ($0.0925$) and the matched-capacity
control ($0.0998$), with the attention models at $0.11$--$0.13$ and the GRU at
$0.1193$. \textbf{Route~B degrades to $0.2077$, the
weakest cross-dataset result among the learned models.} The mechanism is the
cycle coordinate: CALCE CS2\_35 lives for 878 cycles, so the fixed-scale
coordinate reaches $1.76$ at end-of-test, far outside the training range
($\le0.34$ on the NASA cells), and route~B's learned dynamics --- which depend
on that coordinate through the ODE residual --- extrapolate poorly. A
contributing design factor is that the ODE residual is evaluated only at the
training batches' cycle coordinates, not on collocation points sampled across
(and beyond) the training range as in standard PINN practice; the learned
dynamics were therefore never constrained in the coordinate region this
transfer enters. (Under the
earlier, leaked normalization every test trajectory was rescaled into $[0,1]$,
which masked exactly this failure; the corrected protocol exposes it.) A
further caution applies to all models in this transfer: the feature scaler is
fit on NASA, so several CALCE features fall outside the $[0,1]$ training range
(the \texttt{ir\_proxy\_ohm} column even changes physical meaning across
datasets, Section~\ref{subsec:technical_breakdown}), and cross-dataset differences therefore mix model
robustness with feature-shift severity. Two mitigations are identified for
future work: saturating the coordinate at the training maximum, and running the
deployed zeroed-coordinate path (which the footprint profiling already uses)
for out-of-range cells. With only a single CALCE test cell, all cross-dataset
rankings remain indicative; the in-distribution LOCO results are the primary
evidence of this thesis.

\section{Prognostic (PHM) Assessment}
\label{sec:phm}

Beyond pointwise SOH error, the models were assessed with standard Prognostics and
Health Management metrics: the $\alpha$-$\lambda$ accuracy (fraction of the
evaluated life within a $\pm20\%$ cone of true RUL) and Cumulative Relative
Accuracy (CRA). RUL is derived from the predicted SOH trajectory and the EOL
threshold (standard PHM practice), not a separate regressor. One property of
this construction is stated explicitly: the RUL value assigned at cycle $k$ is
computed from the model's full predicted SOH trajectory, whose later portion
requires features measured \emph{after} cycle $k$. The assessment is therefore
a retrospective evaluation of how well each model's estimated trajectory dates
end-of-life, applied identically to every model --- not a true online forecast
issued at cycle $k$, which would additionally require extrapolating the
feature stream. Two protocol notes
are essential. First, cycles after end-of-life are \emph{masked} rather than
scored: an earlier version counted the trivial post-EOL region (where true and
predicted RUL are both zero) as hits, which inflated $\alpha$-$\lambda$; the
corrected protocol evaluates only the informative pre-EOL region, and all values
below use it. Second, the Prognostic Horizon metric degenerates under this
masking (most models exit the $\alpha$-cone near end-of-life, driving PH to
zero), so it is not reported as a comparison axis.

instruments only the linear and recurrent layers, so the

counted --- a bias in the Transformers' favour, which makes the hybrid's
reported latency advantage conservative. Third, the activation-RAM figure is
the largest single intermediate fp32 activation observed in a forward pass, a
proxy that lower-bounds true peak RAM (simultaneously live tensors are not
summed); it is applied identically to every model.

\section{Implementation Caveats}
\label{sec:caveats}

For completeness, the implementation details that bound the claims are recorded
here rather than left implicit:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cycle-coordinate protocol.} An earlier draft normalized the
    cycle coordinate per trajectory, which leaked each test cell's total
    recorded life (the life fraction correlates with SOH at $|r|\ge0.97$). The
    corrected protocol uses a fixed scale ($\mathrm{cycle}/500$) supplied
    identically to every learned model; all results in this chapter use the
    corrected protocol. The residual limitation is extrapolation: on
    trajectories much longer than the training cells the coordinate leaves the
    training range, which is the mechanism behind route~B's cross-dataset
    degradation (Section~\ref{sec:cross_dataset}).
    \item \textbf{Order-independent seeding.} Every model is seeded immediately
    before construction, so each reported result is independent of notebook
    execution order. (Earlier versions seeded only the training loop; model
    initialization then depended on ambient random state, which allowed
    unrelated code changes to shift downstream results.)
    \item \textbf{Quantization and latency.} Int8 sizes are measured (dynamic
    quantization, qnnpack engine, with a measured/estimated flag recorded); the
    M4 latency is analytic and conservative toward the Transformers, whose
    attention matrix products are excluded from the MAC count. On-board
    wall-clock measurement remains future work.
    \item \textbf{Hyperparameter-selection overlap.} Model configurations were
    tuned once on a fixed development fold (test cell B0018, validation B0007)
    and then frozen for the benchmark; both of those cells later serve as
    held-out test cells in the leave-one-cell-out folds. With only four cells a
    fully nested tuning protocol is impractical, so this overlap is disclosed as
    a limitation: the frozen configurations carry some information from cells
    that the benchmark treats as unseen. The same disclosure covers the fixed
    physics weight ($\lambda_{\mathrm{phys}}=0.1$), which was chosen informally
    during development rather than by the recorded search. The recorded search
    itself is a development-phase artifact (a small TPE study plus the locked
    winning configurations, tuned at hidden size 32; the benchmark hybrids run
    hidden size 64 for headroom) rather than a fully scripted reproduction.
    Relatedly, the reduced-protocol ablations retain a slightly earlier base
    configuration (learning rate $0.0018$, physics weight $0.062$) than the
    benchmark models ($0.002$, $0.1$); since those ablations are read only
    qualitatively (they are noise-dominated, see below), the discrepancy does
    not affect any conclusion, but it is recorded here for exactness.
    \item \textbf{Cross-dataset validation windows.} In the cross-dataset run
    the per-cell chronological train/validation split is made on windowed data,
    so up to $\mathrm{seq\_len}-1$ sliding windows straddle the boundary and
    share cycles between train and validation. This mildly optimistic overlap
    affects early stopping in the cross-dataset protocol only; the LOCO
    protocol holds out whole cells and is unaffected.
    \item \textbf{Confidence intervals over correlated runs.} The reported
    $\pm$95\% intervals are computed over the 12 fold$\times$seed runs, which
    are not fully independent (seeds within a fold share the test cell), so the
    intervals are somewhat narrower than fold-level intervals would be; the same
    caution stated for the significance tests applies to them.
    \item \textbf{Evaluation cycle range.} Windowing discards the first
    $\mathrm{seq\_len}-1$ cycles of each test cell, so models with different
    sequence lengths (8--15) are scored on slightly different cycle sets; at the
    margins reported here the effect is small but nonzero.
    \item \textbf{RUL persistence.} EOL \emph{labels} use the 5-cycle persistence
    rule of Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation}; the RUL curves derived
    from \emph{predicted} SOH trajectories at evaluation time use a 3-cycle
    persistence, a slightly more responsive setting applied identically to every
    model, so PHM comparisons between models are unaffected. For clarity, the
    reference (true) RUL in the PHM metrics is likewise re-derived from the
    measured SOH trajectory under the same rule; the label column of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets_implementation} is not used at evaluation time.
    \item \textbf{PHM metric coverage.} $\alpha$-$\lambda$ and CRA are
    computable only where both the true and the predicted trajectory cross the
    EOL threshold; folds where a model never predicts EOL drop out of that
    model's PHM average. This affects all models symmetrically but means the PHM
    columns average over slightly different fold subsets. Post-EOL cycles are
    masked (Section~\ref{sec:phm}).
    \item \textbf{Reduced-protocol ablations.} The 2-fold $\times$ 2-seed
    ablations are demonstrably noise-dominated (their variant rankings changed
    across initialization streams while the full benchmark did not); design
    conclusions are drawn from the full-protocol control row instead.
    \item \textbf{Ablation input asymmetry.} In the reduced-protocol design
    ablation the route-A and physics-off variants are trained \emph{without}
    the shared cycle-coordinate channel (only the route-B and t-channel-control
    variants carry it), whereas in the main benchmark every learned model
    receives it. The ablation rows are therefore not directly comparable to the
    benchmark rows --- one more reason they are read only qualitatively.
    \item \textbf{Window subsampling in the volume sweeps.} The data-volume
    ablation and the data-scarcity stress test subsample training
    \emph{windows}, not contiguous cycles; overlapping windows retain nearly
    full life coverage at low fractions, so these sweeps understate the
    difficulty of a genuinely shorter measurement record.
    \item \textbf{Retrospective RUL evaluation.} As stated in
    Section~\ref{sec:phm}, the PHM metrics score RUL curves derived from each
    model's full predicted SOH trajectory; they measure how well the estimated
    trajectory dates end-of-life in hindsight, not the accuracy of an online
    forecast issued mid-life.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary of Findings}
\label{sec:results_summary}

The measured evaluation supports the following conclusions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Learnable physics (route~B).} Under the leak-free,
    order-independent protocol the learnable ODE-residual attains the lowest
    mean SOH RMSE in the benchmark ($0.0292 \pm 0.0100$) and the highest
    physical consistency among learned models ($0.884$). The matched-capacity
    control settles the attribution: the identical 41k backbone and time input
    without the physics loss scores $0.0377$, so the physics residual --- not
    capacity --- delivers the accuracy gain ($-22.6\%$, on 11 of 12 paired runs
    and three of four cells with the fourth a near-tie, run-level Wilcoxon
    $p=0.021$; Table~\ref{tab:gap}).
    Route~B is better than or equal to the fixed prior (route~A, $0.0382$) on
    every cell.
    \item \textbf{Transformer comparison.} At this data scale, scaling the
    Transformer from 17k to 225k parameters buys no reliable accuracy return
    ($0.0369$ vs.\ $0.0379$, overlapping intervals) while costing $8.7\times$
    the int8 flash and reducing physical consistency. The hybrid's mean
    accuracy advantage over the heavy Transformer ($-21.0\%$, though only 5/12
    individual runs --- the mean gap reflects the Transformer's run-to-run
    variance) is not
    statistically significant; its physical-consistency advantage holds on all
    12 runs and all 4 cells --- both stated descriptively, since four
    independent cells cannot support formal significance.
    \item \textbf{Prognostics under the corrected protocol.} With post-EOL
    cycles masked, PHM metrics are harsh for every model: the non-neural floors
    and the light Transformer calibrate best on $\alpha$-$\lambda$
    ($0.44$--$0.49$), the LSTM leads CRA among the learned models ($0.47$), and
    route~B sits mid-pack ($\alpha$-$\lambda$ $0.37$, CRA $0.17$). Prognostic
    calibration does not by itself separate the hybrid from all alternatives.
    \item \textbf{Negative results, reported as measured.} Uncertainty
    weighting shows no reliable benefit over a fixed weight; the
    reduced-protocol ablations
    are noise-dominated and their earlier readings are withdrawn in favour of
    the full-protocol control; route~B degrades out of distribution ($0.2077$)
    because its cycle coordinate extrapolates beyond the training range; and
    shrinking to the 11k deployment size costs accuracy
    ($0.0292 \rightarrow 0.0469$) with high fold-to-fold variance.
    \item \textbf{Deployment.} The deployed 11k hybrid measures $20.2$\,KB of
    int8 flash (2\% of the Cortex-M4 budget) and $1.25$\,KB of RAM, is
    statistically indistinguishable from the Transformers on accuracy, and
    retains the highest physical consistency of the deployment candidates at
    $3.5\times$--$30\times$ less flash. Its case is plausibility per kilobyte;
    peak accuracy belongs to the benchmark-size model.
\end{itemize}

These outcomes replace the interim report's claim that the hybrid always wins
with a more precise conclusion: at realistic data scales, a learnable physics
residual improves a recurrent backbone where added capacity --- recurrent or
attention --- does not, and it produces the most physically plausible learned
predictions; the framework's remaining weaknesses (coordinate extrapolation
across datasets, accuracy loss at the smallest deployment size) are identified,
explained, and bounded.
